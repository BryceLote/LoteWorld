Something to Point To

A home server, a long weekend, and the things I build when I can't sit still
~10 min read

My job changed on a Monday. The kind of change that is objectively good and still rearranges everything in your head. New structure, new expectations, more autonomy, more money, more pressure. The metrics I used to hide behind were gone. Nobody cared how many calls I made anymore. They cared what I closed. I spent the whole week trying to wrap my head around what that meant and telling myself I would use the weekend to get set up, get organized, get ready to perform on Monday.

That was the plan. Prepare, rest, come back sharp.

I didn't do any of those things.

· · ·

I should back up. The laptop had been sitting in my house for a while. An ASUS ROG gaming laptop from when I ran a set of gyms, retired when I changed careers and replaced by something smaller. Powerful machine. Eight-core processor, dedicated graphics card, a terabyte of storage. Doing nothing.

A few weeks earlier I had moved my Plex media server onto it, which was the first sign that the hardware still had life in it. Then the external hard drive failed. I spent a Saturday fixing it, got it mounted and stable again, and in the process realized the server could be so much more than just a media box. It could be file storage for the house. Ad blocking. Home automation. Game servers for the kids. A real system.

I made the deliberate choice not to start. I know myself well enough by now to see what's coming before it arrives. I knew that the moment I opened that door I would not close it until I had walked through every room. I knew I would hyperfixate. I knew I would stay up too late and neglect the things that actually mattered that weekend. I named the problem, out loud, to myself, and I chose restraint.

That restraint lasted exactly one week.

· · ·

Friday night, the work week let go and I was in the house with nothing structured in front of me. The anxiety about Monday started filling the space before I'd even changed out of my work clothes. Unstructured time is where my brain turns on itself. I can feel it happening, the same way I could feel the hyperfixation coming the week before and chose to set the project aside. I can see the pattern clearly. I can name it. I can explain exactly what's about to happen and why.

I do it anyway.

My hands need something to do. My brain needs a problem it can solve right now, tonight, something that produces a result I can see and point to when it's done. The server was right there. I reached for it the way I always reach for a project when the silence gets too loud.

I started building around 8 PM and I didn't stop until after 1 in the morning.

· · ·

The first problem was that a laptop does not want to be a server. It wants to sleep. Close the lid and it suspends. Leave it idle for too long and it suspends. Let it drop to battery during a power flicker and it suspends. I found three separate systems inside Ubuntu that each independently believed they had the authority to put the machine to sleep, and I had to disable all three before the thing would stay awake with the lid closed.

I was doing all of this from my phone. SSH into the server through an app called Termius, typing commands on a phone keyboard, pasting configuration that the phone kept mangling because YAML files depend on exact spacing and line breaks and a phone clipboard respects neither. I figured out a workaround: write the entire file as a single command with the line breaks coded in as text characters. It didn't matter if the phone flattened everything on the way in; the command reconstructed the correct file when it ran. That one trick made the rest of the build possible.

Docker was the framework underneath all of it. I'd never used it. Each service runs in its own container, defined by a small configuration file, and the whole thing can be destroyed and recreated from that file in seconds. The concept made sense to my brain in a way that messy manual installations never would have. Clean inputs, reproducible outputs, total control. I put the first container up and the pattern locked in.

Plex was already running. I added network file sharing so the external drive showed up on every device in the house. Then DNS-level ad blocking, home automation, monitoring, and encrypted remote access. Each service introduced a new problem with an answer I could find: a port Ubuntu wouldn't surrender, a light that needed to know the difference between motion and human intent, an alert that had to reach me without exposing the system itself.

Every problem gave me a metric I understood: working or not working. Each fix was a small victory. Each one took longer than I thought. Each one kept me somewhere else while my family lived their evening ten feet away.

· · ·

Here is what 1 AM feels like when you're deep in a build and you know you should stop.

It feels like what you're doing is important. It feels like if you don't finish it right now you never will. The rational part of your brain recognizes the familiar ADHD urgency: the conviction that if you stop now, the project will vanish from your life unfinished. You know the urgency is manufactured. You know the project will still be there tomorrow. But the feeling is louder than the knowing. And the feeling has an argument that's hard to beat: when you're done, you will have built something real. You can point to it. You can say, I didn't waste time. I built that.

So you let the feeling win. Again. The same way you let it win on Friday night when you knew better. The same way you'll let it win tomorrow. You can see the pattern. You can name it. You do it anyway.

Even Claude, the AI helping me troubleshoot, had started telling me to go to bed. I kept asking it for the next command.

Alli and I got into bed, and I immediately pulled out my phone and kept working. When the baby woke up after midnight, she got up to handle it and saw me still there, still working on the server. She'd watched me put the kids to bed like I was already somewhere else, go straight back to the project the second the baby was down, and carry it into bed with us.

She didn't lose me to a locked office. I was right there. Physically present the whole time. Just not actually there.

· · ·

Saturday afternoon, all of us sat on the couch watching Young Sheldon. I had suggested it because my family loves The Big Bang Theory, and I thought everyone would enjoy it. I was right. They loved it.

I was on the couch with them, laptop open, working on the server.

At one point everyone busted out laughing. I had missed the joke, but I laughed too without even thinking, like a kid who doesn't know why everyone is laughing but doesn't want to be left out. Alli looked over at me. It wasn't a hard look or even a judgmental one. It was just awareness. She had seen exactly what happened.

It was a small moment. That is how the cost arrives: in small moments that add up.

I made dinner for the family. Put the kids to bed and felt my attention pulling toward the project before their lights were even off. Built until 1:30 AM. Got into bed carrying the same quiet shame as the night before.

· · ·

Sunday morning I built the Minecraft servers before I went outside to do yard work.

I wanted the kids to have something from all the time I'd spent. I loaded their existing saved worlds onto the server so they'd always be there, persistent, no one needing to host from their own device.

I got Nehemiah's world transferred first. When he saw it was up and running he was beside himself. Genuine, uncomplicated joy. He couldn't wait to jump on.

Norah was impressed in the way she's impressed by most things I build, warmly and briefly. She doesn't really care about the server. She cares about playing together. The idea that we could both be in the same world at the same time is what excited her, not the infrastructure underneath it.

· · ·

The system works. It does everything I set out to build. The media library streams to every device. The drive backs up the household's files. The ad blocker is running. The cameras arm and disarm on schedule. The monitoring dashboard shows a row of green. The kids have their Minecraft worlds.

I wrote a reference manual for the whole thing. Thirty pages. Ten chapters. Four appendices. Every decision, every command, every failure and its fix, documented in full. I wanted it all recorded; not just enough to fix something later, but enough to see the complete scope of what I had built. I wanted it specific and permanent. The kind of document you can hand to someone and say, this is what I made, and they would understand every piece of it. The manual is the most thorough thing I have ever written about a project, and I wrote it because the pride I felt in what I had accomplished was so large that I needed somewhere to put all of it.

That pride is real. I built a genuine system doing genuine work for my family, from a laptop that was collecting dust and a set of tools I had never touched before. I am proud of every piece of it.

But Monday came anyway.

It came with the added weight of knowing I chose to distract myself instead of prepare. Knowing I chose that over being present with my family. Knowing that after all of it, after every late night and every service and every line of configuration, I still haven't unclenched.
